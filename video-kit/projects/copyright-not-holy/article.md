Copyright law is a made-up moral rule. Not made-up as in useless—made-up as in invented by statute, not discovered in the moral order.

The Seventh Commandment forbids theft; it does not forbid copying. You can take a man's ox, and that's theft. You can learn his song and sing it, and the ox is still there.

Copyright is a government-granted monopoly—a privilege created to incentivize production, not a natural right rooted in creation. It has terms, exceptions, compulsory licenses, and expiration dates because it is policy, not morality. That is positive law, and positive law can be wise or foolish.

We treat infringement as "piracy" and "stealing," but that's marketing. Calling a thing stealing doesn't make it so. If copyright were intrinsically immoral to violate, the rules would not change every few decades or differ by country.

The real moral duties are: don't defraud, don't plagiarize, honor contracts, and don't claim another's work as your own. Those are commanded. The rest is a legal bargain dressed in moral clothing.

The state may grant limited monopolies; it cannot manufacture sin. So stop baptizing statutory monopolies as righteousness. Copyright may be prudent. It is not holy.
